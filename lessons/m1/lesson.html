Vectors and coordinate frames

Before a robot can move its hand somewhere, it needs a way to say where "somewhere" is. This lesson gives you that language: arrows that carry a length and a direction, and frames that say where you are measuring from.

About 45 minutes · 2 labs and 2 small interactive scenes · 5 missions

In this lesson you will:

  • drag arrows and add them head to tail, the same way a robot arm's links add up;
  • measure an arrow's length and direction with numbers;
  • describe one point from two different frames and switch between the two descriptions;
  • meet the right-handed frame the whole course uses.

Every scene works with a mouse, a finger or the keyboard: click a scene or press Tab to reach it, move with the arrow keys (hold Shift for small steps), and press Enter to switch to the next handle.

1. Arrows with a length and a direction

Think of an instruction like "walk 3 steps east and 2 steps north". It says how far and which way, but not where to start: you can follow it from your front door or from the bus stop. An arrow that carries exactly this, a length and a direction and nothing else, is called a vector.

Because a vector doesn't remember where it starts, you can slide it anywhere on the page without changing it. Two arrows are the same vector when they have the same length and point the same way.

I still catch myself thinking of a vector as "the arrow from here to there". It's only the "how far and which way" part. Where you draw it is your choice.

Vectors add up head to tail: put the start of the second arrow on the tip of the first, and the sum is the arrow from the first start to the last tip. Try it.

That last mission is the idea behind how a robot arm finds where its hand is, which you'll meet in Part 3: each link of the arm is a vector, and the hand sits at the sum of all of them.

Under the hood: adding vectors with numbers

Write a vector by how far it goes along x and along y, \(a = (a_x, a_y)\). Then

\[a + b = (a_x + b_x,\; a_y + b_y).\]

Head to tail on the grid and part by part in numbers give the same answer, and the order doesn't matter: \(a + b = b + a\). The next section gives the two parts their proper name.

2. Components, length and direction

Any arrow on a grid can be split into how far it goes along x and how far along y. These two numbers are its components, and we write them in brackets: (3, 4) means 3 along x and 4 along y. A component is negative when the arrow goes left or down.

The two components and the arrow form a right triangle, so the arrow's length, also called its magnitude, comes from Pythagoras: the arrow (3, 4) is 5 long. A length is never negative.

Divide a vector by its own length and you get a unit vector: same direction, length one. (3, 4) divided by 5 is (0.6, 0.8). Unit vectors say "this way" without saying "how far", and in the next section they become the axes of a frame. Drag the tip and watch the purple unit vector: it never gets longer or shorter, it only turns.

Under the hood: length and unit vector
\[|v| = \sqrt{v_x^2 + v_y^2}, \qquad \hat v = \frac{v}{|v|} = \left(\frac{v_x}{|v|},\; \frac{v_y}{|v|}\right).\]

The zero vector (0, 0) has length 0 and no direction, so it has no unit vector: the division would be by zero. Try dragging the tip onto the centre.

3. A frame: an origin and two axes

So far the grid has quietly told us where things are. Make that explicit and you have a coordinate frame: a point called the origin, plus one unit vector for each axis, x and y, at right angles to each other. We give frames names in curly brackets, like {A}.

The arrow from a frame's origin to a point P is P's position vector in that frame. Its components are what we usually call the coordinates of P. To say which frame we measured from, we write the frame's name as a small superscript in front: AP is P's position vector in {A}. The course slides and Craig's textbook use the same notation, so it's worth getting used to now.

4. One point, two frames

A robot carries many frames at once: one at its base, one on each link, one at the hand, one on the camera. The same point gets a different description in each. Here are two frames, {A} and {B}, that point the same way but sit in different places.

To get from {A}'s origin to P, you can go straight there, or first go to {B}'s origin and then on to P. Both routes end at P, so head to tail: AP = APBORG + BP, where APBORG is {B}'s origin seen from {A}.

I read the superscript as "seen from": AP is P seen from {A}. It keeps me from mixing up which frame a number belongs to.

Going the other way, from {A} to {B}, you subtract instead: BP = AP − APBORG. This only works because {A} and {B} point the same way. Frames that are turned against each other need one more ingredient, a rotation, and that is what Part 2 is about.

Under the hood: changing frames by adding
\[{}^{A}P = {}^{A}P_{BORG} + {}^{B}P, \qquad {}^{B}P = {}^{A}P - {}^{A}P_{BORG}.\]

Both hold component by component, as long as the axes of {A} and {B} are parallel.

Where is the cup?

A robot has a camera bolted above its base. The camera spots a cup and reports where it is in the camera's own frame: 0.3 m along the camera's x axis and 0.1 m along its y axis. To reach for the cup, the robot needs its position in the base frame.

What else does the robot need to know, and how does it combine the numbers?

Think about it for a minute before opening the answers.

Possible answers
  • Where the camera is. Measure the camera's origin in the base frame and add the cup's position in the camera frame, exactly as in the lab above.
  • Line the frames up. Mount the camera so that its frame is the base frame. Then nothing needs converting, but a camera sitting inside the robot's base can't see much.
  • How the camera is turned. If the camera is tilted, adding is not enough: you also need its rotation. That's the general case, and it's Part 2.
What was actually done

Robotics does the first and the third together: every object gets its own frame, and descriptions are chained from frame to frame, base to camera to cup. When the frames point the same way, as here, the chain is plain vector addition.

Some of the earliest computer vision already worked like this. Lawrence Roberts' 1963 MIT thesis, Machine Perception of Three-Dimensional Solids, recovered 3D objects from a camera image by relating object models and the camera through coordinate transformations. Richard Paul's 1981 textbook, Robot Manipulators: Mathematics, Programming, and Control (MIT Press), built robot programming on chains of such frame transformations.

5. Into 3D: right-handed frames

A real robot works in space, so its frames get a third axis, z, at right angles to both x and y. That leaves a choice: z can point to one side of the xy plane or to the other. The two choices are mirror images of each other, and no amount of turning makes one into the other.

The convention almost everyone uses, and the one this course uses throughout, is the right-handed frame: point the fingers of your right hand along x, curl them towards y, and your thumb points along z.

Yes, everyone in a robotics lecture holds up their right hand at this point. Do it anyway; I still do.

Flip one axis and the frame turns left-handed; flip a second and it's right-handed again. Mixing up the two conventions is a classic source of sign errors, so whenever a frame looks odd, check it with your hand.

Check yourself: eight common misconceptions

What we covered

  1. A vector is a length and a direction. Where you draw it doesn't matter.
  2. Vectors add head to tail, or component by component, and the order doesn't matter.
  3. The length comes from Pythagoras. Dividing by it gives the unit vector, which the zero vector doesn't have.
  4. A frame is an origin plus unit axes. AP is P's position vector in {A}.
  5. For frames that point the same way, AP = APBORG + BP.
  6. The course uses right-handed frames: x, y, and the thumb gives z.

Open questions

Can three vectors add up to zero?

Yes: when they form a closed triangle head to tail, you end where you started. A robot arm whose hand touches its own base is an example.

What changes in section 4 if {B} is turned against {A}?

Adding alone no longer works: P's components in {B} are measured along turned axes, so they first have to be turned into {A}'s directions. That's the rotation matrix, coming in M.3 and Part 2.
