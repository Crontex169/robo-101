A two-link arm

This page shows every block a lesson can use, so a new lesson can start as a copy of it. It walks through a planar arm with two links: you move its joints, see where the hand ends up, and find out which points it can reach at all.

About 10 minutes · 1 lab and 1 interactive scene · 3 missions

In this lesson you will:

  • turn the two joints and put the hand on a target, then predict what bending the elbow does;
  • change the second link's length and watch the reachable ring grow and shrink.

1. Joint angles and where the hand ends up

Our arm lies flat on a table. A shoulder joint at the base turns the first link, and an elbow joint turns the second link relative to the first. Two numbers, the joint angles θ₁ and θ₂, decide the whole pose.

Going from joint angles to the position of the hand is called forward kinematics. The links simply add up, one after the other. Try it: drag the elbow or the hand, or use the sliders, and put the hand on the green target.

The first time I did this, I only turned the shoulder and wondered why the hand never got closer. The shoulder can't change the distance to the base: that's the elbow's job.

Guessing angles by hand works, but it's slow.

Let the computer find the angles

You just searched for θ₁ and θ₂ by hand, watching the hand creep toward the target. A robot controller has to do the same many times a second, and for arms with six joints, not two.

How could a computer find joint angles that put the hand on a given target?

Think about it for a minute before opening the answers.

Possible answers
  • Solve the triangle. The shoulder, the elbow and the target form a triangle whose three sides you know: the two link lengths and the distance to the target. Trigonometry gives the angles exactly, and it shows there are two answers, elbow up and elbow down.
  • Take small steps. Nudge each joint a little, watch how the hand moves, and keep turning the joints in the direction that brings the hand closer. It works for any arm but needs many steps.
  • Pull the chain. Put the hand on the target, drag the elbow after it so the link keeps its length, then pull the chain back so the shoulder sits on the base again. Repeat until it settles. No angles at all during the search.
What was actually done

All three ideas are real methods. In his 1968 Stanford thesis, Donald Pieper showed that a six-joint arm whose last three joint axes meet in one point can be solved exactly with formulas, much like our triangle. That result is one reason so many industrial arms have such a "spherical wrist" (Pieper, 1968).

A year later, Daniel Whitney described resolved motion rate control: don't solve for the angles at all, but compute small joint speeds that move the hand the way you want, again and again (Whitney, 1969).

The chain-pulling idea came much later, from computer animation: FABRIK by Andreas Aristidou and Joan Lasenby (Aristidou and Lasenby, 2011). You'll meet the first two methods properly in Parts 4 and 5.

Next, let's see which points the hand can reach at all.

2. The reachable ring

Swing both joints through every angle and the hand sweeps out a ring around the base. This set of points is the arm's workspace. Its outer edge is where the arm is fully stretched, its inner edge where it's folded back on itself. Change the second link's length and watch the ring.

3. Adding up the links

Each link points in the direction of all the angles before it added together. The hand's position is the sum of the two link vectors. With our lengths, 0.5 m and 0.4 m, the hand can be at most 0.9 m from the base and at least 0.1 m.

I expected the arm to reach everything inside its circle. The hole in the middle surprised me: with a shorter second link, the hand can never fold back onto the base.

Under the hood: forward kinematics of a two-link arm
\[\begin{aligned} x &= L_1\cos\theta_1 + L_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2),\\ y &= L_1\sin\theta_1 + L_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2). \end{aligned}\]

The workspace is the ring between radii \(|L_1 - L_2|\) and \(L_1 + L_2\).

Related papers

The papers behind the ideas on this page. Each card says in one sentence what the paper brought; open "More details" for how it connects to our two-link arm.

A Kinematic Notation for Lower-Pair Mechanisms Based on Matrices (may need a subscription)

J. Denavit, R. S. Hartenberg · Journal of Applied Mechanics 22(2) · 1955

Any chain of joints and links can be described with four numbers per joint, and the pose at the end of the chain is the product of one matrix per joint.

More details

Our two-link arm is easy: add two vectors. With six joints in 3D, each turning about a different axis, you need careful bookkeeping. Denavit and Hartenberg, writing about mechanisms in general before industrial robots existed, proposed describing every joint-to-joint step with the same four numbers and one 4×4 matrix, then multiplying the matrices along the chain.

This course uses a later variant of their convention, the "modified" one from Craig's textbook. It comes in Part 3.

The Kinematics of Manipulators Under Computer Control

D. L. Pieper · PhD thesis, Stanford University · 1968

Arms whose last three joint axes meet in one point have an exact, formula-based solution to "which angles put the hand here?"

More details

Our arm has a formula solution too: the triangle from the research question gives the elbow angle from the law of cosines and the shoulder angle from the triangle's other angles, with two answers, elbow up and elbow down.

Pieper looked at six-joint arms, where such formulas usually don't exist, and found arm shapes for which they do. The thesis also builds a model of a six-joint arm and plans motions around obstacles. It was published as Stanford AI Memo AIM-72; the link is a free scan.

Resolved Motion Rate Control of Manipulators and Human Prostheses (may need a subscription)

D. E. Whitney · IEEE Transactions on Man-Machine Systems 10(2) · 1969

Instead of solving for the angles, ask which small joint speeds move the hand the way you want, and repeat.

More details

The table that links joint speeds to hand speed is the Jacobian, the subject of Part 5. For our arm it's a 2×2 table that changes with the pose. Whitney turned it into a way to steer manipulators and arm prostheses: the operator asks for a hand velocity, and the controller inverts the table to get joint speeds.

When the arm is fully stretched or folded, the table can't be inverted and the method breaks down. Later papers found ways around that.

FABRIK: A Fast, Iterative Solver for the Inverse Kinematics Problem

A. Aristidou, J. Lasenby · Graphical Models 73(5) · 2011

Move the hand to the target, drag each joint after it, then drag the chain back to the base; a few passes are usually enough.

More details

FABRIK stands for Forward And Backward Reaching Inverse Kinematics. During the search it never computes an angle: each step just places a joint on the line towards its neighbour, at the right link length. It was written for computer animation, where long chains such as spines and fingers have to follow targets in real time, and it can respect joint limits.

Try it in your head on our arm: hand onto the target, elbow pulled along, shoulder pulled back onto the base.

What we covered

  1. Forward kinematics turns joint angles into the hand's position by adding up the links.
  2. A two-link arm reaches a ring: no farther than L₁ + L₂, no closer than |L₁ − L₂|.

Open questions

What shape is the workspace when both links have the same length?

The inner radius |L₁ − L₂| becomes zero, so the ring turns into a full disc: the hand can reach the base itself.
